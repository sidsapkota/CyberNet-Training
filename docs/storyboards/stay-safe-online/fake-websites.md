# Fake Websites (`fake-websites`)

- **Module:** 2 · Spot the Scam · Pro (order 4 after the Scam Calls split)
- **The one idea:** A fake site can copy a logo and get a padlock, but only the web address shows who
  really owns the page.
- **The story:** Sam's friend Alex, Alex's phone and one "Your Bank" sign-in page (the `fake-website`
  scene), with Sam helping Alex check it. Each card changes one thing: Alex sends the page, Sam looks
  closer, Alex nearly types the password, the address gets read, then other addresses, an extra word at
  the front, and finally a password manager that stays quiet.
- **Cards:** 8 · difficulty: easy (3 Predict), easy (5 Easy win), medium (6 Twist), stretch (7 Real life)
- **From today's lesson:** keeps `meet-the-page` (Play), `who-gets-it` (Predict), `read-the-address`
  (Name it, now one picture of the address bar labelled instead of a three-example list) and `recap`
  (Wrap). `manager-stays-quiet` becomes the Real life card (multiple choice, one step). New:
  `alex-sends-a-page` (Hook), `real-address` (Easy win), `word-in-front` (Twist). Dropped:
  `bank-or-not` (its four addresses are split between the Easy win, the Twist and the quiz),
  `spot-the-site` (the Play and Name it already cover the rush and the PIN box; the lesson's one idea is
  the address) and `typed-it-in` (recovery is taught in the free module 4: Signs of a Hack and Getting
  Help).
- **Builds on:** Scam Emails ("Rushing you", "Asks for secrets", skip the link and go there yourself)
  and Two-Step Sign-In (password managers). The Real life card's situation states the password-manager
  fact itself, because today's Two-Step lesson only says it in an explanation.
- **Needs building:** a **hook card** (a full-width picture, one line, tap to start). Until it exists,
  card 1 can be a `reveal` on the scene's address bar. New pictures: Alex's chat, the labelled address
  bar, the three address bars and the password-manager screen.

## 1 · Hook
- **Situation:** Alex messages Sam: "Is this page really Your Bank?"
- **Picture:** Sam's phone, a chat with Alex. Alex's bubbles: "Email said my card's locked", "Tapped
  the link and this opened. Real??" Under them, a screenshot thumbnail of a sign-in page with a big
  "Your Bank" logo. The thumbnail has a soft pulse ring.
- **Action:** Tap the screenshot to open it.

## 2 · Play
- **Situation:** It looks just like Your Bank. Tap each part to look closer.
- **Picture:** the existing `fake-website` scene, full width: padlock and address bar
  ("https://yourbank.example.login-check.example/signin"), the "Your Bank" logo, the red banner "Your
  account is locked! Sign in within 10 minutes to unlock it.", boxes for Username, Password and Card
  PIN, and the footer. Five parts pulse twice. Callouts, pinned inside the scene:
  - **Logo:** "Looks just like Your Bank's. But anyone can copy a logo in minutes."
  - **Padlock:** "It means what you type is scrambled on the way. Scam sites get padlocks too."
  - **Banner:** "10 minutes! Rushing you, like the scam email."
  - **Card PIN box:** "Asks for secrets. A real bank never asks for your card PIN on a web page."
  - **Address:** "Read the name just before the very last .example: login-check. That's who owns this
    page."
- **Action:** Tap each glowing part (Continue unlocks after all five). Not graded.

## 3 · Predict
- **Situation:** Alex types the password into this page. Who gets it?
- **Picture:** the page with "••••••••" in the Password box and the "Sign in" button pressed; a small
  key leaves the page. Below, three tappable objects: a bank building with the Your Bank logo ("Your
  Bank: it's their logo"); a laptop with a "login-check" sticker ("login-check"); a padlocked safe
  ("Nobody: the padlock keeps it safe").
  → **Reveal:** the key travels inside a little padlocked tube (scrambled on the way) and comes out at
  the login-check laptop, where it pops open: "Got it". Your Bank's building stays untouched.
- **Action:** Tap who gets the password.
- **Answers:** Your Bank (building) · login-check (laptop) · nobody (safe)
- **Correct:** login-check
- **Right:** The address says login-check owns the page, so it gets the password. The padlock only
  scrambles it on the way.
- **1st wrong:** Which part of the page showed who really owns it?
- **2nd wrong:** login-check gets it: the web address shows who owns the page, whatever the logo says.

## 4 · Name it
- **Says:** A fake site can copy a logo, but not the real **web address**. The owner is the name just
  before the very last ending, like `.example`.
- **Picture:** the same address bar, drawn big across the card:
  `yourbank.example.login-check.example/signin`. Under it, tags: "yourbank.example." (dimmed) "Decoy";
  "login-check" (cyan box) "Owner"; ".example" (the last one) "Last ending"; "/signin" (dimmed) "Just a
  page on the site". A small inset below: "Your Bank's real address: yourbank.example".

## 5 · Easy win
- **Situation:** Alex closes the fake page. Which web address is really Your Bank's?
- **Picture:** three browser address bars stacked as big tappable bars, each with a padlock:
  `yourbank.example/signin`, `login-check.example/signin`, `yourbank-secure.example/signin`. The inset
  "Your Bank's real address: yourbank.example" stays at the top.
- **Action:** Tap Your Bank's real address.
- **Answers:** the three address bars
- **Correct:** `yourbank.example/signin`
- **Right:** The name before the last ending is exactly yourbank, so it's Your Bank's.
- **1st wrong:** Find the very last ending in each, then read the name just before it.
- **2nd wrong:** `yourbank.example/signin`. The others are owned by login-check and yourbank-secure, which
  isn't the same name.

## 6 · Twist
- **Situation:** This web address starts with login: login.yourbank.example. Who owns it?
- **Picture:** one address bar, big: `login.yourbank.example`, with a padlock. Below, three tappable tags:
  a Your Bank building "Your Bank"; a stranger's laptop "Someone called login"; a question mark "Nobody
  can tell".
  → **Reveal:** the bar lights up from the right: ".example" (Last ending), "yourbank" (Owner, cyan),
  "login." (dimmed: "a part of Your Bank's site").
- **Action:** Tap who owns it.
- **Answers:** Your Bank · someone called login · nobody can tell
- **Correct:** Your Bank
- **Right:** The name just before the last ending is yourbank, so it's Your Bank's own page.
- **1st wrong:** Start from the right: find the last ending, then the name just before it.
- **2nd wrong:** Your Bank owns it. Words in front don't change the owner; only the name before the last
  ending counts.

## 7 · Real life
- **Situation:** Your password manager only fills in on real web addresses. Here, it won't.
- **Picture:** your phone showing a page that looks exactly like Your Bank's sign-in (same logo, same
  boxes; the address bar is cut off at "yourbank.exa…"). The password box is empty, and a small key icon
  pop-up says "No saved passwords for this site". Below, three tappable answers.
- **Action:** Tap what it tells you.
- **Answers:** "This page isn't on Your Bank's real address" · "The password manager is broken, so type
  it in" · "Your Bank has turned password managers off today"
- **Correct:** "This page isn't on Your Bank's real address"
- **Right:** A password manager reads the whole web address for you, so a quiet one is a warning sign.
- **1st wrong:** Where does a password manager fill in, and what does it check first?
- **2nd wrong:** It isn't Your Bank's real address. Close it and open the Your Bank app or a bookmark
  instead.

## 8 · Wrap
- **Takeaway:** Logos can be copied and scam sites get padlocks too; only the web address shows who
  owns a page.
- **Try this:** Bookmark the sign-in pages you use, and open them from there.
